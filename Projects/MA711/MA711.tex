\begin{definition}[Metric Space]\label{dfn:0}
	Let $X$ be a set, and let $d : X\times X \to \mathbb{R}$ be a map satisfying
	\begin{itemize}
		\item For any $x\in X$, $d(x,x)=0$.
		\item (Positivity) For $x\neq y$, then $d(x,y)>0$.
		\item (Symmetry) For $x,y\in X$, $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$.
		\item (Triangle Inequality) For $x,y,z\in X$, $d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z)$.
	\end{itemize}
	Then the pair $(X,d)$ is called a metric space, and $d$ is called a metric on $X$.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Topological Space]\label{dfn:1}
	Let $X$ be a set, and let $\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{P} (X)$ be a collection of subsets of $X$. Then the pair $(X,\mathcal{T} )$ is a topological space if the following axioms are satisfied:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\varnothing,X\in \mathcal{T} $.
		\item $\mathcal{T} $ is closed under countably infinite unions.
		\item $\mathcal{T} $ is closed under finitely many intersections.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
An example of a topological space is $\mathbb{R}$, under the topology induced by the euclidean metric. That is, for any $x\in\mathbb{R}$, define $B_x(r)=\left\{ y\in\mathbb{R} \mid \left\lVert x-y \right\rVert <r \right\} $. Then define $\mathcal{B} =\left\{ B_x(r) \mid x,r\in\mathbb{R} \right\} $. A valid topology $\mathcal{T} $ on $\mathbb{R}$ is simply any $B\in \mathcal{B} $, or any set that can be represented as either a finite intersection or countably infinite union of sets in $\mathcal{T} $.
\begin{remark}
	The definition of $\mathcal{T} $ is very similar to that of a $\sigma $-algebra, and hence the definition of a topological space is similar to that of a measurable space. Furthermore, a topological space is simply an abstraction of a metric space. Instead of necessarily having open balls, we need only have open sets now.
\end{remark}
\begin{definition}[Open Set]\label{dfn:2}
	Let $(X,\mathcal{T} )$ be a topological space. Then any $S\in \mathcal{T} $ is called an open set.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Continuous Function]\label{dfn:3}
	Let $(X,\mathcal{T} )$ and $(Y,\mathcal{S} )$ be topological spaces. Then a map $f : X \to Y$ is called continuous if for all open sets $V\subseteq Y$, the preimage $f^{-1}(V)=\left\{ x\in X \mid f(x)\in V \right\} $ is open in $X$.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Homeomorphism]\label{dfn:4}
	Let $(X,\mathcal{T} )$ and $(Y,\mathcal{S} )$ be topological spaces, and let $f : X \to Y$ be a function satisfying the following properties:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $f$ is bijective.
		\item $f$ is continuous.
		\item $f^{-1}$ is continuous.
	\end{enumerate}
	Then we say $f$ is a homeomorphism.
\end{definition}
Homeomorphisms are isomorphisms in the category of topological spaces. That means we can treat homeomorphic spaces as being precisely the same object, in the same way we would treat isomorphic groups as the same group. For example, suppose we have a statement about $X$. Using the fact that a homeomorphism is not only a bijection, but its continuous inverse means you can think of it as a bijection of the topology itself, we can treat the topologies as the same, and make the same statement about $f(X)=Y$.
\begin{definition}[Neighborhood]\label{dfn:5}
	Let $(X,\mathcal{T} )$ be a topological space, and let $x\in X$. Then an open subset $N\subseteq X$ is called a \textbf{neighborhood} of $x$ if $x\in N$.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Hausdorff]\label{dfn:6}
	Let $(X,\mathcal{T} )$ be a topological space. Then we say the space is Hausdorff if for all distinct $x,y\in X$, there exist neighborhoods $N_x,N_y\subset X$ of $x$ and $y$, respectively, such that $N_x\cap N_y=\varnothing$.
\end{definition}
Once again, a great example of a Hausdorff space is $\mathbb{R}$. Let $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$ be distinct points. Using our previous topology given by the metric, we know that any open subset $(a,b)\subset \mathbb{R}$ with $a\neq b$ is open in $\mathbb{R}$. Let $\left\lVert x-y \right\rVert =n$. Then the sets $B_x(n / 4),B_y(n  /4)$ are by definition neighborhoods of $x$ and $y$, but they are also obviously disjoint.

\section{$L^p$ Spaces}
We have previously explored vector spaces at length in linear algebra, and possibly even in abstract algebra. However, we want to specialize our explorations of linear algebra to function spaces, which is a large part of the field of \textbf{functional} \textbf{analysis}. Recall the definition of a vector space.
\begin{definition}[Vector Space]\label{dfn:2.1}
	Let $V$ be a set, $\mathbb{F}$ a field, and define the maps $+ : V\times V \to V$ and $\cdot : \mathbb{F}\times V \to V$. Then the tuple $(V,\mathbb{F},+,\cdot )$ is a vector space if and only if
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $(V,+)$ is abelian.
		\item There exists $1\in\mathbb{F}$ such that $1\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}$ for all $\mathbf{v}\in V$.
		\item For all $a,b\in\mathbb{F}$ and $\mathbf{v}\in V$, $(ab)\mathbf{v}=a(b \mathbf{v})$.
		\item For all $a\in\mathbb{F}$ and $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in V$, $a(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})=a \mathbf{u}+a \mathbf{v}$.
		\item For all $a,b\in\mathbb{F}$ and $\mathbf{v}\in V$, $(a+b)\mathbf{v}=a \mathbf{v}+b \mathbf{v}$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
We will usually call a space over $\mathbb{F}$ an $\mathbb{F}$-vector space. One important type of vector space is the $L^p$ space, which we will now define. First, we need to define the notion of ``almost everywhere'' in a precise, measure-theoretical manner.
\begin{definition}[Almost Everywhere]\label{dfn:2.2}
	Let $(X,\Sigma ,\mu )$ be a measure space, and let $P(x)$ be a property of $x$ for all $x\in X$. Then $P(x)$ is said to hold \emph{almost everywhere} if there exists a measureable set $N\in \Sigma $ such that $\mu (N)=0$, and $P(x)$ holds for all $x\in X\setminus N$.

	Moreover, let $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$. Define the essential infimium as the maximum lower bound $a$ such that $f\geq a$ almost everywhere. Symbolically,
	\[
		\essinf = \left\{ a\in\mathbb{R} \mid \mu \left\{ x\in X \mid f(x)>a \right\} =0+ \right\} 
	.\]
	Define the essential supremium as the minimum upper bound $b$ such that $f\leq b$ almost everywhere. Symbolically,
	\[
		\esssup=\left\{ b\in\mathbb{R} \mid \mu \left\{ x\in X \mid f(x)<b \right\} =0 \right\} 
	.\]
\end{definition}
These definitions are important for the formalism of the infinity norm, for reasons that might be made clear later. I don't know.
\begin{definition}[$p$-norm]\label{dfn:2.3}
	Let $(X,\Sigma ,\mu )$ be a measure space, and let $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$. Define $\left\lVert \cdot  \right\rVert _p$ as
	\[
		\left\lVert f \right\rVert _p\left( \int_X \left| f(x) \right| ^p \,\mathrm{d} x \right) ^{1 /p}
	.\]
	Moreover, define the infinity norm $\left\lVert \cdot  \right\rVert _\infty$ as
	\[
		\left\lVert f \right\rVert _{\infty}=\esssup f
	.\]
	That is, $\left\lVert f \right\rVert _\infty=a$ if $f(x)\leq a$ almost everywhere, with respect to the measure $\mu $.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[$L^p$-space]\label{dfn:2.4}
	Let $(X,\Sigma ,\mu )$ be a measure space, and let $p\in\mathbb{R}^+\cup\left\{ +\infty \right\} $. Define $L^p(X)$ as the set
	\[
		L^p(X)=\left\{ f : X \to \mathbb{R} \mid \left\lVert f \right\rVert _p<\infty \right\} 
	.\]
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Convex Function]\label{dfn:2.5}
	A function $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is called \textbf{convex} if it is convex up. A more formal definition of this is for any $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\lambda \in[0,1]$, $f(\lambda x+\left( 1-\lambda  \right) y)\leq \lambda f(x)+\left( 1-\lambda  \right) f(y)$.
\end{definition}
The motivation behind the formalism of a convex function is not as involved as it seems. Recall that a line between points $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ can be parametrized as the vector function $\mathbf{r}(t)=t\mathbf{x}+(1-t)\mathbf{y}$ for $t\in[0,1]$. This is the same idea, except we need only parametrize the interval $[x,y]$, and we are using $\lambda $ rather than $t$.

Now consider the actual definition itself. Notice that $f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda )y)$ will only give us values in $f[x,y]$, and thus we only get a specific line segment. If the function is convex up, this line segment will be above the graph of $f$ for all $\lambda $, hence the definition.
\begin{theorem}[Minkowski's Inequality]\label{thm:2.1}
	Let $f,g\in L^p(X)$ for $p\geq 1$ and $X$ a measure space. Then
	\[
		\left\lVert f+g \right\rVert _p\leq \left\lVert f \right\rVert _p+\left\lVert g \right\rVert _p
	.\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{lemma}[Young's Inequality]\label{lma:2.1}
	Let $\alpha ,\beta >0$, and let $\lambda \in[0,1]$. Then
	\[
		\alpha^\lambda \beta ^{1-\lambda }\leq \lambda \alpha +\left( 1-\lambda  \right) \beta 
	.\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{theorem}[Holder's Inequality]\label{thm:2.2}
	Let $X$ be a measure space, and let $f\in L^p(X)$ and $g\in L^q(X)$. If $\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1$, then
	\[
		\int_X fg \,\mathrm{d} \mu \leq \left\lVert f \right\rVert _p\left\lVert g \right\rVert _q
	.\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proposition}\label{pro:2.1}
	Let $(X,\Sigma ,\mu )$ be a measure space. Then $\left( L^p(X),\mathbb{R},+,\cdot  \right) $ is a vector space under the usual definitions.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	Trivial except for closure under addition. Let $X$ be a measure space, and let $f,g \in L^p(X)$. By theorem \ref{thm:2.1}, $\left\lVert f+g \right\rVert_p\leq \left\lVert f \right\rVert _p+\left\lVert g \right\rVert _p$. Since $f,g\in L^p(X)$, we know $\left\lVert f \right\rVert _p,\left\lVert g \right\rVert _p$ are finite, and thus their sum is finite. Therefore, $f+g\in L^p(X)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prp:2.3}
	A normed vector space $(V,\left\lVert \cdot  \right\rVert )$ forms a metric space $(V,d)$ with $d(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\left\lVert \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} \right\rVert $.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	By proposition \ref{prp:2.2}, definition \ref{dfn:2.6}, and definition \ref{dfn:2.1}, we have $d(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})=\left\lVert \mathbf{0}-\mathbf{0} \right\rVert =\left\lVert \mathbf{0} \right\rVert =\mathbf{0}$.

	Now we show positive-definiteness. Let $\mathbf{v}\in V$. By definition \ref{dfn:2.1}, $\left\lVert \mathbf{0} \right\rVert =\left\lVert \mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v} \right\rVert $. By lemma \ref{lma:2.2}, $\left\lVert \mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v} \right\rVert =\left\lVert \mathbf{v}+(-1)\mathbf{v} \right\rVert $. By definition \ref{dfn:2.6},
	\begin{align*}
		\left\lVert \mathbf{v}+(-1)\mathbf{v} \right\rVert &\leq \left\lVert \mathbf{v} \right\rVert +\left\lVert (-1)\mathbf{v} \right\rVert \\
								   &=\left\lVert \mathbf{v} \right\rVert +\left| -1 \right| \left\lVert \mathbf{v} \right\rVert \\
								   &=\left\lVert \mathbf{v} \right\rVert +\left\lVert \mathbf{v} \right\rVert \\
								   &=2\left\lVert \mathbf{v} \right\rVert 
	.\end{align*}
	Therefore, $\left\lVert \mathbf{0} \right\rVert \leq 2\left\lVert \mathbf{v} \right\rVert $. Since $\left\lVert \mathbf{0} \right\rVert =0$, we have $\left\lVert \mathbf{v} \right\rVert \geq 0$ for all $\mathbf{v}\in V$. By definition \ref{dfn:2.6}, $\left\lVert \mathbf{v} \right\rVert =\mathbf{0}$ if and only if $\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{v}$. Therefore, for $\mathbf{x}\neq \mathbf{y}$, by our knowledge of group theory we know that $\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\neq \mathbf{0}$. Therefore, $\left\lVert \mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y} \right\rVert >0$ for all $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in V$.

	Next, we show symmetry. Let $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in V$. By lemma \ref{lma:2.2}, $\left\lVert \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} \right\rVert =\left\lVert \mathbf{u}+(-1)\mathbf{v} \right\rVert $. By definition \ref{dfn:2.6},
	\[
		\left\lVert \mathbf{u}+(-1)\mathbf{v} \right\rVert =\left| -1 \right| \left\lVert (-1)\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} \right\rVert =\left\lVert (-1)\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} \right\rVert 
	.\]
	By lemma \ref{lma:2.2} and definition \ref{dfn:2.6},
	\[
		\left\lVert (-1)\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} \right\rVert =\left\lVert \mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u} \right\rVert 
	.\]
	Therefore, $d(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=d(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u})$.

	Finally, we show the triangle inequality holds. Let $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in V$. By definition \ref{dfn:2.6} and definition \ref{dfn:2.1},
	\begin{align*}
		\left\lVert \mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z} \right\rVert &=\left\lVert \mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{0} \right\rVert \\
							       &=\left\lVert \mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z} \right\rVert \\
							       &=\left\lVert \left( \mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y} \right) +\left( \mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z} \right)  \right\rVert\\
							       &\leq \left\lVert \mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y} \right\rVert +\left\lVert \mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z} \right\rVert 
	.\end{align*}
	Therefore, $d$ is a metric on $V$.
\end{proof}

\section{Operator Spaces}
We now want to generalize our study of vector space beyond the $L^p$ spaces, although they will continue to be an important example. We will focus our studies on normed linear spaces and functions between vector spaces. We will also exclusively study real spaces.

In linear algebra, a map $A$ between two spaces $X\to Y$ is generally called an \textbf{operator}.
\begin{definition}[Linear Operator]\label{dfn:2.10}
	Let $X,Y$ be vector spaces. A map $A : X \to Y$ is called \textbf{linear} if for all $\mathbf{x}\in X$, $A\left( \mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y} \right) =A(\mathbf{x})+A(\mathbf{y})$, and for all $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}$, $A(\alpha \mathbf{x})=\alpha A(\mathbf{x})$.
\end{definition}

An important question to ask is whether an operator is continuous, specifically with respect to the metric.
\begin{definition}[Continuous Function (Metric)]\label{dfn:2.11}
	Let $(X,d_X),(Y,d_Y)$ be metric spaces. A function $f : X \to Y$ is called continuous at a point $c\in X$ if for all $\varepsilon >0$, there exists a $\delta >0$ such that for all $x\in X$ satisfying $d_X(c,x)<\delta $, we have $d_y(f(c),f(x))<\varepsilon $.
\end{definition}
There is a theorem concerning this result, but first we must make a few definitions.
\begin{definition}[Bounded]\label{dfn:2.12}
	Let $\left( X,\left\lVert \cdot  \right\rVert _X \right) $ and $\left( Y,\left\lVert \cdot  \right\rVert _Y \right) $ be normed linear spaces. Then a map $A : X \to Y$ is called \textbf{bounded} if there exists some $\alpha \in\mathbb{R}$ such that $\left\lVert A \mathbf{x} \right\rVert _Y \leq \alpha \left\lVert \mathbf{x} \right\rVert _X$ for all $\mathbf{x}\in X$.
\end{definition}
We might then want to define the norm of an operator as this $\alpha $ scalar.
\begin{definition}[Operator Norm]\label{dfn:2.13}
	Let $X$ and $Y$ be normed linear spaces with norms $\left\lVert \cdot  \right\rVert _X,\left\lVert \cdot  \right\rVert _Y$, respectively. Let $A : X \to Y$ be a linear mapping. Define 
	\[
		\left\lVert A \right\rVert =\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\frac{\left\lVert A \mathbf{x} \right\rVert_Y }{\left\lVert \mathbf{x} \right\rVert_X }
	.\]
\end{definition}
\begin{proposition}\label{prp:2.4}

\end{proposition}

Now we want to focus on infinite-dimensional spaces. We will begin by extending the idea of a linear combination to infinite dimensions.
\begin{definition}[Infinite Linear Combination]\label{dfn:2.18}
	Let $X$ be a normed linear space\footnote{NLS required for a limit to exist}, let $\left\{ \mathbf{x}_\alpha  \right\}_{\alpha \in A}\subset X$ be some collection of vectors, and let $\left\{ c_\alpha  \right\}_{\alpha \in A}\subset \mathbb{R}$ be a corresponding collection of scalars. Define the linear combination
	\[
		\sum_{\alpha \in A}c_\alpha \mathbf{x}_\alpha  
	\]
	as any sum that converges. This means the following:

	If $\left| A \right| <\infty$, then the linear combination is the same as the finite case, and the meaning is thus clear.

	If $\left| A \right| =\aleph_0$ is a countable indexing set, then the linear combination is defined as
	\[
		\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n \mathbf{x}_n \triangleq \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{N} c_n \mathbf{x}_n
	.\]
	Notice that we specify an order in this case, since series are not necessarily commutative and associative in the general case.

	If $\left| A \right| >\aleph_0$ is an uncountable indexing set, then since it does not make sense to talk about an uncountable sum, for the linear combination to exist we require all but a countable subset of $A$ satsify $c_\alpha \mathbf{x}_\alpha =\mathbf{0}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Linear Independence, Spanning, Basis]\label{dfn:2.19}
	Let $X$ be a normed linear space, and let $\mathcal{C} =\left\{ \mathbf{x}_\alpha  \right\}_{\alpha \in A}\subseteq X$ be a collection of vectors.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item We say $\mathcal{C} $ is \textbf{linearly} \textbf{independent} if no $\mathbf{x}_k\in \mathcal{C} $ is equal to a (infinite) linear combination of the other elements of $\mathcal{C} $.
		\item We say $\mathcal{C} $ \textbf{spans} $X$ if every vector in $X$ can be written as a (infinite) linear combination of vectors in $\mathcal{C} $.
		\item We say $\mathcal{C} $ is a \textbf{basis} for $X$ if it is linearly independent and spans $X$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Set of Linear Functionals]\label{dfn:2.20}
	Let $X$ be a normed linear space. Then the set of linear functionals $X\to \mathbb{R}$ is denoted $X^\#$, which is pronounced ``X sharp''. 
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Kernel]\label{dfn:2.21}
	Let $X,Y$ be vector spaces, and $A : X \to Y$ be a linear map. Then the \textbf{kernel} of $A$ is defined as
	\[
		\ker A=\left\{ \mathbf{x}\in X \mid A \mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} \right\} 
	.\]
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Direct Sum]\label{dfn:2.22}

	If $Y\cap Z=\left\{ \mathbf{0} \right\} $, we denote this as $\mathbf{Y}\oplus Z$, and call this the \textbf{direct} \textbf{sum} of $Y$ and $Z$.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Codimension]\label{dfn:2.23}

	\[
		\operatorname{codim}Y=\dim Z \iff Y\oplus Z=X

\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Weak Topology]\label{dfn:2.26}
	Let $X$ be a space, and let $F$ be any collection of maps $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$. Then the \textbf{weak} \textbf{topology} induced by $F$ on $X$ is the weakest topology on $X$ such that every $f\in F$ is continuous.

	If $X$ is a normed linear space and no collection $F$ is referenced, we automatically take $F$ to be the dual space $X^*$.
\end{definition}

The weak topology is important, but very unintuitive. So we need to develop some intuition for what the weak topology looks like. A neighborhood $N$ in the weak topology requires some $\varepsilon >0$ and a collection $\Psi $ of linear functionals, and is defined as
\[
	N_{\varepsilon ,\Psi }=\left\{ x\in X \mid \forall \psi \in \Psi : \left| \psi (x)<\varepsilon  \right|  \right\}
.\]

For example, consider $\psi : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$ defined as $\mathbf{x}\mapsto x_1+2x_2-3x_3$. Notice that $\nabla \psi =(1,2,-3)$, and $\psi =\varepsilon $ on some plane perpendicular to $\nabla \psi $. The neighborhood is then the region between these planes.
\begin{definition}[Partially Homogeneous Subadditive Functional]\label{dfn:2.27}
	Let $X$ be a normed linear space, and let $p : X \to \mathbb{R}$. Then we say $p$ is a \textbf{partially} \textbf{homogeneous} \textbf{subadditive} \textbf{functional} if $p(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})\leq p(\mathbf{x})+p(\mathbf{y})$ and $p(\alpha \mathbf{x})=\alpha p(\mathbf{x})$.
\end{definition}
One example of a partially homogeneous subadditive functional is the norm of a space.
\begin{definition}[$p$-bounded]\label{dfn:2.28}
	Let $X$ be a normed linear space, and let $p$ be a partially homogeneous subadditive functional. Then a linear functional $\psi : X \to \mathbb{R}$ is called $p$-bounded if $\phi (x)\leq p(x)$ for all $x$.
\end{definition}

Out final goal with operator spaces concerns the extension of linear functionals onto larger spaces. More precisely, we want to prove the Hans-Banach theorem. First, we will need the Axiom of Choice.
\begin{mdframed}[linewidth=1pt]
	\textbf{Axiom of Choice}: Let $\mathcal{A} $ be a collection of nonempty sets. Then a new set can be created by including precisely one $a\in A$ for all $A\in \mathcal{A} $.
\end{mdframed}
The Axiom of Choice is equivalent to Zorn's lemma, which is the real machinery we need.
\begin{definition}[Partial Order]\label{dfn:2.29}
	A set $A$ is \textbf{partially} \textbf{ordered} if there exists a relationship $\prec$ on $A$ such that for all $a,b,c\in A$,
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $a\prec b$ and $b\prec c$ implies $a\prec c$.
		\item $a\prec b$ and $b\prec a$ if and only if $a=b$.
	\end{itemize}
	We call $A$ a \textbf{poset}.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Total Order]\label{dfn:2.30}
	A partially ordered set $(A,\prec)$ is called \textbf{totally} \textbf{ordered} if for all $a,b\in A$, either $a\prec b$ or $b\prec a$.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Upper Bound]\label{dfn:2.31}
	Let $(A,\prec)$ be a poset and $E\subset A$. Then some $a\in A $ is an upper bound of $E$ if for all $e\in E$, $e\prec a$.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Maximal Element]\label{dfn:2.32}
	A maximal element of a poset $(A,\prec)$ is some $a\in A$ succh that $b\prec a$ for all $b\in A $.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}[Zorn's Lemma]\label{thm:2.7}
	Let $(X,\prec)$ be a poset such that every subset of $X$ that is totally ordered has an upper bound. Then $X$ is a maximal element.
\end{theorem}
We will not prove Zorn's Lemma, not for lack of ability but rather lack of time. We will use Zorn's lemma in the second part of the proof of the Hans-Banach theorem to generalize the results of the first part to infinite dimensional vector spaces. The first part is summarized in the Hans-Banach lemma.
\begin{lemma}[Hans-Banach]\label{lma:2.6}
	Let $X$ be a normed linear space, let $Y\subset X$ be a subspace, and let $\psi : Y \to \mathbb{R}$ be a $p$-bounded linear functional for some fixed partially homogeneous subadditive functional $p$. Let $\mathbf{z}\in X\setminus Y$, and define $Y_1 = \operatorname{span}\left( Y\cup \left\{ \mathbf{z} \right\}  \right)  $. Then $\psi $ can be extended to $Y_1$ and still be $p$-bounded.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	It suffices to show that simply letting $\psi (\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{R}$ and claiming $\psi $ is still linear is well defined, and implies that $\psi $ is still $p$-bounded for at least some choice of $\psi (\mathbf{z})$.
\end{proof}
One remark about the Hans-Banach lemma is that $\dim \operatorname{span}(Y\cup \left\{ \mathbf{z} \right\} ) =\dim Y+1$. This means by induction, the statement immediately generalizes to all finite dimensional spaces. That is, for $X$ finite dimenional and $\psi : Y\subset X \to \mathbb{R}$, we can extend $\psi $ to the entirety of $X$. We must now show it holds in the infinite dimensional case, which is the Hans-Banach theorem.
\begin{theorem}[Hans-Banach]\label{thm:2.8}
	Let $X$ be a normed linear space, let $Y\subset X$ be a subspace, and let $\psi : Y \to \mathbb{R}$ be a $p$-bounded linear functional for some fixed partially homogeneous subadditive functional $p$. Then $\psi $ can be extended to $X$ and still be $p$-bounded.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Consider $\overline{f} $, the set of all $p$-bounded linear functionals $\eta $ on all subspaces $Y_\eta $ of $X$ that extend $\psi $. Remark that for all $\eta $, $Y_\eta \supset Y$. Consider the partial order $\prec$ on $\overline{f} $ such that $\eta_1\prec \eta_2$ if and only if $\eta_2$ extends $\eta _1$. We then need to use Zorn's lemma to show there exists a maximal element of $\overline{f} $, which would be some $p$-bounded linear functional $\psi : X \to \mathbb{R}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Inner Product]\label{dfn:2.33}
	Let $\mathcal{H} $ be an inner product space. A map $\left<\cdot ,\cdot  \right>: \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{H}  \to \mathbb{R}$ is called an \textbf{inner} \textbf{product} if for all $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in \mathcal{H} $,

We will now use theorem \ref{thm:2.16} to derive Fourier series in a rigorous manner. Consider $X=[-\pi ,\pi ]$, specifically as viewed on the unit circle, where we say $-\pi =\pi $. Let
\[
	\mathscr{S} =\left\{ 1 \right\} \cup \left\{ \cos (nx),\sin (nx) \mid n\in\mathbb{Z} \right\} 
.\]
We want these functions to be orthonormal under the $2$-norm and associated inner product. Note
\[
	\left\langle 1,1 \right\rangle =\int_{-\pi }^{\pi } 1 \,\mathrm{d} x=2\pi 
.\]
A similar analysis of other functions shows that we need to modify our set to be
\[
	\mathscr{S} =\left\{ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi } } \right\} \cup \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi } }\left\{ \cos (nx),\sin (nx) \mid n\in\mathbb{Z} \right\} 
.\]
Then $\mathscr{S} $ is an orthonormal set under the $L^2$ inner product. Define $\mathfrak{S} =\operatorname{span}\mathscr{S}  $ under finite linear combinations, and note that any $f\in \mathfrak{S} $ has the form
\[
	f(x)=a_0+\sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n\cos (nx)+b_n\sin (nx)
.\]
It can be shown that $\mathfrak{S} $ forms an algebra, and since we take $-\pi=\pi $, it can be shown that $\mathfrak{S} $ separates points. By theorem \ref{thm:2.16}, $\mathfrak{S} $ is dense in $C(X,\mathbb{R})$.

Note that $C(X,\mathbb{R})$ has the $\infty$-norm, and since it is continuous, I believe this is equivalent to the supremum norm:
\[
	\left\lVert f \right\rVert_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in X}\left| f(x) \right| 
.\]
Therefore, for all $f\in C(X,\mathbb{R})$ and for all $\varepsilon >0$, there exists some $p\in \mathfrak{S} $ such that $\left\lVert f-p \right\rVert <\varepsilon $. Therefore, $\mathfrak{S} $ spans $C(X,\mathbb{R})$.

What if we now go from the $\infty$-norm to the $2$-norm? Well, it turns out that if $\mathfrak{S} $ is dense in $(C(X,\mathbb{R}),d_{\infty})$, then it is also dense in $(C(X,\mathbb{R}),d_2)$. Apparently this is easy to show. We can now consider $L^2[-\pi ,\pi ]$. It's also easy to show that $C(X,\mathbb{R})$ is dense in $L^2(X)$, so since $\mathfrak{S} $ spans $X$, we have $\mathfrak{S} $ dense in $L^2(X)$.

Let $f\in L^2(X)$. We have
\[
	f=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n\cos (nx)+b_n\sin (nx)
.\]
Since we require $\mathfrak{S} $ be orthonormal, we have
\[
	a_n=\frac{1}{\pi }\left\langle f(x),\cos (nx) \right\rangle ,\qquad b_n=\frac{1}{\pi }\left\langle f(x),\sin (nx) \right\rangle

Final remarks for the class include applications of functional analysis in quantum mechanics. If you have a quantum system of particles, then you can represent the set of all possible states in your system as a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H} $, and vectors $\Psi  \in \mathcal{H} $ are infinite dimensional. The time dependence of the system in quantum mechanics behaves according to the Schrodinger Equation:
\[
	\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial t} =\mathbf{\hat{H}}\Psi 
.\]
The term $\mathbf{\hat{H}}$ is called the Hamiltonian operator, and it is an operator on $\mathcal{H} $. This is then a system of linearish differential equations, and solutions of this system are eigenfunctions and eigenvalues, and thus there are an infinite number of them.
