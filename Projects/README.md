# Projects


This folder contains many projects I worked on over my undergraduate years. Many of these were independent readings, and some were notes prepared for talks and presentations I gave at the BU math club.

- [**Higher topos theory (Lurie)**](./HTT-Lurie): This is the most challenging reading project I have attempted, and it is still ongoing. I started reading HTT in the beginning of the 2025 Fall semester, and so far have covered chapter 1 and the beginnings of chapter 2. I intend to complete the book through chapter 5, possibly with the ommission of chapter 3, by the end of summer 2026. I have so far gained a basic knowledge of quasi-categories, and am currently working through the section on their homotopy theory. I anticipate reading more of Hovey, and possibly parts of Goerss & Jardine, before completing HTT. I believe my notes are fairly decent, and definitely improve as I progress through the book and gain a more complete understanding of the objects I am working with.
- [**Model categories (Hovey)**](./Model-Categories-Hovey): I began reading Hovey's *Model categories* late in the 2025 summer break, and completed chapter 1. This covered the definition and basic facts of a model category, Quillen adjunctions, derived and total derived functors, and Quillen equivalences. This chapter provided me with a solid foundation in abstract homotopy theory and familiarity with lifting properties, which has made it possible to begin reading Lurie's *Higher topos theory* and work through some theorems of simplicial homotopy. I intend to read the sections of chapter 2 on the small object argument and the model structure for chain complexes, as well as chapter 3, which discusses Quillen's model structure for simplicial sets. I think these notes are some of the best in this folder.
- [**Categories for the working mathematician (Mac Lane)**](./Categories-MacLane): Over the beginning of the 2025 summer break, I read chapters 1-7, 9, and 10 of Mac Lane's *Categories for the working mathematician*. Chapters 1-5 covered the basics of category theory; chapter 6 covered monads and their algebras; chapter 7 covered monoidal categories; chapter 9 covered (co)ends and special limits, and chapter 10 covered Kan extensions. This was my second experience with an independent reading project, and I am extremely happy with how it turned out. I'm extremely confident applying the language of category theory in my work, including topics that confused me while I was reading the book. Mac Lane has written a wonderful reference book, but it was horrible to learn from and has terrible notation and I would not recommend anyone ever read it cover to cover. However, I like doing stupid things so this same rule does not apply to me :3
- [**Categorical homotopy theory (Riehl)**](./Categorical-Homotopy-Theory-Riehl): While reading Chapter 10 of Mac Lane, I found that Riehl's *Category theory in context* had a more transparent and thorough introduction to Kan extensions and their use in homotopy theory, which was expanded upon in her *Categorical homotopy theory*. Intrigued, I attempted to read her book for the next month or so of the 2025 summer break. It was quite difficult and I did not get past the first chapter, however it provided a first introduction to simplicial sets, and I realized I needed to learn about the theory of simplicial sets, and about model category theory. I also learned a lot of enriched category theory, and was able to solidify my knowledge of basic category theory by immediately applying it to complex problems.
- [**Algebra: Chapter 0 (Aluffi)**](./Algbera): Over the 2024 winter break and 2025 spring semester, I read chapters 1, 2, 3, 8, and part of 9 of Aluffi's *Algebra: Chapter 0*, which covered (in order) basic category theory, group theory, ring and basic module theory, multilinear algebra, and homological algebra. This was my first experience with independent reading, and although I certaintly could have taken better notes and gotten more out of the book, the knowledge of multilinear algebra I've gained has been invaluable and has formed a sufficient working knowledge for many advanced applications.
- [**MA711 (notes)**](./MA711): In the 2024 Fall semester, I sat in on the last third of MA711, the graduate level course in real analysis that BU has. We covered some basic topology the first day, and then spent the remainder of the time introducing functional analysis, including $L^p$ spaces, Banach and Hilbert spaces, and some important theorems. I was able to follow the course fairly well by making liberal use of wikipedia to gain a working knowledge of measure theory and the Lebesgue integral. The class gave me a good introduction to topology, as well as a solid working knowledge of $L^p$ spaces and functional analysis.
- [**Exposition on simplicial sets (notes)**](./exposition_on_simplicial_sets): I wrote this late in the 2025 summer break. I wrote it as if I was writing an expository paper, and even refer to it as a paper, but was actually writing notes as I was essentially learning the material as I went. Writing this paper provided me with basic knowledge of what simplicial sets are, what horns and boundaries are, and some important theorems about them. It was sufficient knowledge to begin reading Lurie's *Higher topos theory*, however I would not call it sufficient knowledge of simplicial sets in general, as I had much to learn about their homotopy theory.
- [**Abelian categories (talk)**](./abelian-categories-talk): I gave a talk on abelian categories as part of the Fall 2025 colloquium on homological algebra, hosted by the [Society of Mathematics](https://societyofmathematics.github.io), a branch of the BU math club. The intended audience was people who know linear algebra, and have been to the colloquium in the past, hence having a knowledge of basic homological algebra. My talk covered the definitions of **Ab**-categories, additive categories, abelian categories, homology in abelian categories, and the proof of functoriality of homology. My extended notes discuss these topics, then introduce derived functors from a homotopical perspective.
