\section{$\Ab $-categories}\label{sec2}

Let $\mathcal{C} $ be a category. In order to do homological algebra in $\mathcal{C} $, we need to be able to at the very least construct \emph{chain complexes} and \emph{homology}. We will begin by constructing chain complexes, which are considerably simpler.

Let's review the definition of chain complexes in the category $k$-$\Vect$ of $k$-vector spaces. A \emph{chain complex} $(V_{\bullet} ,d_{\bullet} )$ is a collection $\left\{ V_i \right\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z} } $ of $k$-vector spaces, and a collection $\left\{ d_i \right\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z} } $ of $k$-linear maps
\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
\cdots \ar[" d_{n+2}  ", r]  & V_{n+1} \ar[" d_{n+1}  ", r]  & V_n\ar[" d_n ", r]  & V_{n-1} \ar[" d_{n-1}  ", r]  & \cdots
\end{tikzcd}\]
such that $d_{i-1} \circ d_i=0$ for all $i$. We would like to elevate this definition to an arbitrary category $\mathcal{C} $. Luckily, the definition is already largely categorical, since we can simply take each $V_i$ to be an object of $\mathcal{C} $, and each $d_i$ to be a morphism $V_i\to V_{i-1} $ in $\mathcal{C} $. The main issue is the condition that $d_{i-1} \circ d_i=0$. In $k$-$\Vect$, the zero-morphism $0 : V \to W$ is the map which sends each $v\in V$ to the additive identity $0\in W$. Unfortunately, objects in arbitrary categories don't have additive identities, so we need to find a new characterization of the zero morphism which is more categorical. The way we do this is by recalling that the hom-set $k$-$\Vect(V,W)$ is an \emph{abelian group}.

\begin{definition}\label{2.1}
	An \emph{abelian group} is a set $G$ together with an operation $+$ such that
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item (closure) For all $g,h\in G$, we have $g+h\in G$;
		\item (associativity) For all $g,h,k\in G$,
			\[
				(g+h)+k=g+(h+k);
			\]
		\item (identity) There exists an element $0\in G$ such that for all $g\in G$,
			\[
				0+g=g+0=g;
			\]
		\item (inverse) For all $g\in G$, there is some element $-g\in G$ such that
			\[
				g+(-g)=(-g)+g=0;
			\]
		\item (commutativity) For all $g,h\in G$,
			\[
				g+h=h+g
			.\]
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}\label{2.2}
	For those familiar with linear algebra, an abelian group is simply a vector space but \emph{without scalars}.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}\label{2.3}
	Let $V,W$ be $k$-vector spaces. Then $k$-$\Vect(V,W)$ is an abelian group. In particular, if $\varphi ,\psi : V \to W$ are $k$-linear maps, then $\varphi +\psi $ is the $k$-linear map defined by
	\[
		(\varphi +\psi )(v)=\varphi (v)+\psi (v),
	\]
	and the additive identity is the zero map $0: V \to W$ given by $0(v)=0$.
\end{proposition}

The proof of \cref{2.3} is not difficult, and an even stronger result is proven in basic coures, which is that $k$-$\Vect(V,W)$ is a $k$-vector space. However, the result tells us something quite interesting: if the hom-sets $\mathcal{C} (x,y)$ of our category $\mathcal{C} $ have an abelian group structure, then we can define the zero morphism as the additive identity of this set. This motivates \cref{2.4}:

\begin{definition}\label{2.4}
	Let $\mathcal{C} $ be a category. We say $\mathcal{C} $ is an $\Ab $\emph{-category} if for all $x,y\in \mathcal{C} $, the hom-set $\mathcal{C} (x,y)$ has an \emph{abelian group structure} (we always denote the group operation by $+$), and if composition distributes over addition. Concretely, this means that if $f,g : x \to y$ and $h : y \to z$ are morphisms in $\mathcal{C} $, then
	\[
		h\circ (f+g)=(h\circ f)+(h\circ g)
	.\]
\end{definition}

It is likely not yet clear why we require composition to distribute over addition, but we will see later why this is an important condition.

\begin{remark}\label{2.5}
	If the reader is familiar with enriched category theory, then they will likely have encountered this as an example before. On the off chance that they have not, we note that an $\Ab $-category in the sense of \cref{2.4} is precisely the underlying category of an $(\Ab ,\otimes ,\mathbb{Z} )$-enriched category, where we impose the group structure by the natural isomorphism $\Ab (\mathbb{Z} ,-)\cong U$ for $U : \Ab  \to \Set $ the forgetful functor.
\end{remark}

\begin{notation}\label{2.6}
	Let $\mathcal{C} $ be an $\Ab $-category. We denote the additive identity of $\mathcal{C} (x,y)$ by $0$, and will call it the zero morphism. We will often not specify the domain and codomain of a zero morphism, and will leave it implicit.
\end{notation}

\begin{definition}\label{2.7}
Let $\mathcal{C} $ be an $\Ab $-category. A \emph{chain complex} $(c_{\bullet} ,d_{\bullet} )$ in an $\Ab $-category is a collection $\left\{ c_i \right\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z} } $ of objects of $\mathcal{C} $,a nd a collection $\left\{ d_i \right\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z} }$ of morphisms of $\mathcal{C} $
\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
\cdots \ar[" d_{n+2}  ", r]  & c_{n+1} \ar[" d_{n+1}  ", r]  & c_n\ar[" d_n ", r]  & c_{nn-1} \ar[" d_{n-1}  ", r]  & \cdots
\end{tikzcd}\]
such that $d_{i-1} \circ d_i=0$ for all $i$. The morphisms $d_{\bullet} $ are called \emph{differentials} or \emph{boundary morphisms}. We will usually denote a chain complex by $c_{\bullet} $, and will leave the notation for the boundary morphisms implicit.
\end{definition}
