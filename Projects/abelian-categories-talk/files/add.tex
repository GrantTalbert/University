\section{Additive categories}

The next step up from an $\Ab $-category is an \emph{additive category}. An extremely important object in homological algebra is the trivial $k$-vector space $0$. The trivial $k$-vector space is used as the ``start'' and ``end'' of many chain complexes, including short exact sequences, and also characterizes when there is no homology in a complex. To have a good theory of homological algebra, we should require that our categories have some kind of ``zero object''. Luckily, regular category theory already provides a good definition.

\begin{definition}\label{3.1}
	A \emph{zero object} or \emph{null object} of a category $\mathcal{C} $ is an object $0\in \mathcal{C} $ which is both initial and terminal.
\end{definition}

Indeed, $0\in\Vect_k$ is both initial and terminal. Additionally, given $V\in \mathcal{C} $ an $\Ab $-category with a zero object, the unique maps $0\to V$ and $V\to 0$ must be the zero map since abelian groups must have an identity. This is once again the same situation that we have in $\Vect_{k} $.

The next definition we introduce is largely motivated by $\Vect_k$. Many constructions and proofs in homological algebra require the following criteria, but we will unfortunately not see any in these notes. To motivate this notion, we present a fascinating theorem.

\begin{proposition}\label{3.2}
	Let $\mathcal{C} $ be an $\Ab $-category, and $x,y\in \mathcal{C} $. If a product $x\times y$ of $x,y$ exists, then a coproduct $x\amalg y$ also exists, and there is an isomorphism $x\times y \cong x\amalg y$. More generally, finite products and coproducts coencide. Dually, if $x\amalg y$ exists, then so does $x\times y$, and $x\times y \cong x\amalg y$.
\end{proposition}
The proof requires a simple lemma.
\begin{lemma}\label{3.3:NEW}
	Let $\mathcal{C} $ be an $\Ab $-category, $x,y\in \mathcal{C} $, and $f : x \to y$. Then $f\circ 0=0$ and $0\circ f=0$ for all zero morphisms 0.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	By \cref{2.4}, composition distributes over addition, and by \cref{2.1} we have $0=0+0$. We then have
	\[
		f\circ 0=f\circ (0+0)=(f\circ 0)+(f\circ 0)
	.\]
	We then subtract $f\circ 0$ from both sides, and note that $(f\circ 0)-(f\circ 0)=0$ since they cancel. This yields
	\[
		0=f\circ 0
	.\]
	The other statement follows in the same way.
\end{proof}


\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{3.2}]
	We first consider the case of the empty (co)product. Let $*\in \mathcal{C} $ be terminal. Then note that the only morphism $*\to *$ must be 0 since $\mathcal{C} (*,*)$ is a singleton. Now let $c\in \mathcal{C} $, and let $f : *\to c$. At least one morphism must always exist, since $\mathcal{C} (*,c)$ is an abelian group, and the empty set is not a group. Then $f=f\circ 1_* = f\circ 0=0$, where the final step follows by \cref{3.3:NEW}. Thus we have $f=0$ for all $f : * \to c$, meaning that $\mathcal{C} (*,c)$ is a singleton. Hence $f$ is initial. This proves the statement for the empty product and coproduct.

	Now let $x,y\in \mathcal{C} $ have a product $x\times y$ in $\mathcal{C} $. We establish some important compatibilities of the $\Ab $-structure with the product. Given a diagram of solid arrows
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
	 & x \\
	z \ar[" f ", ur] \ar[" g "', dr] \ar[" (f\text{,} g) ", dashed, r]  & x\times y \ar[" \pi _x "', u] \ar[" \pi _y ", d]  \\
	 & y
	\end{tikzcd}\]
	we always denote the unique arrow, as indicated by the dashed arrow, by $(f,g)$. By commutativity of the diagram, $\pi _x \circ (f,g)=f$ and $\pi _y \circ (f,g)=g$. In particular, it is the unique morphism with this property.

	Let $z\in \mathcal{C} $, and let $f_1,f_2 : z \to x$ and $g_1,g_2 : z \to y$. We claim 
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:1}
		(f_1,g_1)+(f_2,g_2)=(f_1+f_2,g_1+g_2).\tag{$\star $}
	\end{equation}
	Indeed, note that since composition distributes over addition,
	\[
		\pi _x\circ ((f_1,g_1)+(f_2,g_2))=(\pi _x \circ (f_1,g_1))+(\pi _x \circ (f_2,g_2))=f_1 + f_2,
	\]
	\[
		\pi _y\circ ((f_1,g_1)+(f_2,g_2))=(\pi _y \circ (f_1,g_1))+(\pi _y \circ (f_2,g_2))=g_1 + g_2
	.\]
	The claim follows by uniqueness of the relevant arrow.

	We also claim that $(1_x,0)\circ \pi _x + (0,1_y)+\pi _y = 1_{x\times y} $. Indeed, let $f : z \to x$ and $g : z \to y$ be any morphisms. Then consider the diagram
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 3em, column sep = 3em]
	 &  & x \\
	 & x \ar[" 1_x ", ur] \ar[" 0 "', dr] \ar[" (1_x\text{,} 0) ", dashed, r]  & x\times y \ar[" \pi _y ", d] \ar[" \pi _x "', u]  \\
	z \ar[" f ", ur] \ar[" g "', dr] \ar[" (f\text{,} g) ", dashed, r]  & x\times y \ar[" \pi _x "', u] \ar[" \pi _y ", d]  & y \\
	 & y &  \\
	\end{tikzcd}\]
	Noting that $f\circ 1_x=f$ and $0\circ \pi _x \circ (f,g)=0$ by \cref{3.3:NEW}, we see that this diagram witnesses the commutativity of the product diagram
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 4.5em, column sep = 6em]
	 & x \\
	z \ar[" f ", ur] \ar[" 0 "', dr] \ar[" (1_x\text{,} 0)\circ \pi _x \circ (f\text{,} g) ", dashed, r]  & x\times y \ar[" \pi _x "', u] \ar[" \pi _y ", d]  \\
	 & y
	\end{tikzcd}\]
	In particular, we see the equality $(1_x,0)\circ \pi _x \circ (f,g)=(f,0)$. The same logic yields $(0,1_y)\circ \pi _y \circ (f,g)=(0,g)$. Recall by \eqref{eq:1} that $(f,0)+(0,g)=(f,g)$. This implies
	\begin{align*}
		(f,g)&=((1_x,0)\circ \pi _x \circ (f,g))+((0,1_y)\circ \pi _y\circ (f,g))\\
		     &=((1_x,0)\circ \pi _x+(0,1_y)\circ \pi _y)\circ (f,g)
	.\end{align*}
	In particular, postcomposition $((1_x,0)\circ \pi _x+(0,1_y)\circ \pi _y)_*$ is the identity map on $\mathcal{C} (x\times y,-)$. Since the Yoneda embedding is fully faithful, it reflects identities, and thus
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:2}
		(1_x,0)\circ \pi _x + (0,1_y)\circ \pi _y = 1_{x\times y}.\tag{$\star\star $}
	\end{equation}

	The identity \eqref{eq:2} highlights the importance of the arrows $(1_x,0)$ and $(0,1_y)$. Note that these define inclusions $x\to x\times y$ and $y\to x\times y$. We will now see that they yield a coproduct structure on $x\times y$. Let $f : x \to z$ and $g : y \to z$. Define $\varphi \coloneqq f\circ \pi _x + g\circ \pi _y$. We claim that $\varphi $ renders the diagram commutative:
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
	x \ar[" (1_x\text{,} 0) "', d] \ar[" f ", dr]  &  \\
	x\times y \ar[" \varphi  ", r]  & z \\
	y \ar[" g "', ur] \ar[" (0\text{,} 1_y) ", u]  & 
	\end{tikzcd}\]
	This is a simple computation:
	\begin{align*}
		\varphi \circ (1_x,0) &= (f\circ \pi_x +g\circ \pi _y)\circ (1_x,0)\\
				      &=f\circ \pi _x \circ (1_x,0) + g \circ \pi _y \circ (1_x,0)\\
				      &=f\circ 1_x + 0\\
				      &=f,\\
		\varphi \circ (0,1_y)&=(f\circ \pi _x + g\circ \pi _y)\circ (0,1_y)\\
				      &=f\circ \pi _x \circ (1_x,0) + g \circ \pi _y \circ (0,1_y)\\
				      &=0+g\circ 1_x\\
				      &=g
	.\end{align*}
	Uniqueness follows by the identity \cref{eq:2}. Suppose there exists $\psi : x\times y \to z$ such that the diagram
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
	x \ar[" f ", dr] \ar[" (1_x\text{,} 0) "', d]  &  \\
	x\times y \ar[" \psi  ", r]  & z \\
	y \ar[" g "', ur] \ar[" (0\text{,} 1_y) ", u]  & 
	\end{tikzcd}\]
	commutes. Then
	\begin{align*}
		\varphi &= \varphi \circ 1_{x\times y} \\
			&=\varphi \circ ((1_x,0)\circ \pi _x+(0,1_y)\circ \pi _y)\\
			&=\varphi \circ (1_x,0)\circ \pi _x + \varphi \circ (0,1_y)\circ \pi _y\\
			&=f \circ \pi _x + g\circ \pi _y\\
			&=\psi \circ (1_x,0)\circ \pi _x + \psi \circ (0,1_y)\circ \pi _y\\
			&=\psi \circ ((1_x,0)\circ \pi _x + (0,1_y)\circ \pi _y)\\
			&=\psi \circ 1_{x\times y} \\
			&=\psi 
	.\end{align*}
	Thus $\varphi =\psi $ is unique, and $x\times y=x\amalg y$ is a coproduct.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{3.3}
	Let $\mathcal{C} $ be a category. Two objects $x,y\in \mathcal{C} $ have a \emph{biproduct} when their product and coproduct coencide. We then call both the product and coproduct a \emph{biproduct} and denote it by $x\oplus y$.
\end{definition}

In practice, we rarely actually call the biproduct a biproduct, and simply call it a product or coproduct when appropriate. We also rarely use the notation $\oplus $ outside of \emph{additive categories}, which we will shortly introduce.

The category $\Vect_k$ has finite products and coproducts, and by \cref{3.2} they coencide. The product of two $k$-vector spaces $V\times W$ is the same as their coproduct $V\amalg W$, so we call it a \emph{direct sum} and denote it by $V\oplus W$. Direct sums are extremely useful in the theory of $k$-vector spaces, and more generally the theory of $R$-modules, so it would be nice if our $\Ab $-categories had finite biproducts as well. This motivates \cref{3.4}.

\begin{definition}\label{3.4}
	An $\Ab $-category $\mathcal{C} $ is an \emph{additive} category if it has a zero object and binary biproducts. Equivalently, it must have a zero object and binary products (or coproducts), since products \emph{are} coproducts in $\Ab $-categories by \cref{3.2}.
\end{definition}
