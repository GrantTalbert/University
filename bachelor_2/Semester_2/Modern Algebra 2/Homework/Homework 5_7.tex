\section*{Problem 1}
Let $r$ be a root of $f(x)$ in $\mathbb{C} $, viewed as an extension field of $\mathbb{Q} $, and note that $r\neq 0$:
\[
	0^4+0^3-0^2-0+1=1\neq 0
.\]
Therefore, since $\mathbb{C} $ is a field, $-1/r$ exists. We have
\begin{align*}
	\left( -\frac{1}{r}  \right) ^4+\left( -\frac{1}{r}  \right) ^3-\left( -\frac{1}{r}  \right) ^2-\left( -\frac{1}{r}  \right) +1&=\frac{1}{r^4} -\frac{1}{r^3} -\frac{1}{r^2} +\frac{1}{r} +1\\
																       &=\frac{1}{r^4} -\frac{r}{r^4} -\frac{r^2}{r^4} +\frac{r^3}{r^4} +\frac{r^4}{r^4} \\
																       &=\frac{1-r-r^2+r^3+r^4}{r^4} \\
																       &=\frac{r^4+r^3-r^2-r+1}{r^4} \\
																       &=\frac{f(r)}{r^4} \\
																       &=\frac{0}{r^4} \\
																       &=0
.\end{align*}
Therefore, $-1/r$ is a root of $f$.
\section*{Problem 2}
By problem 1, roots come in pairs of the form $(r,-1/r)$. By a theorem I found on Google, the product of all roots of $f$ is the constant term of $f$. By the method of proof by the professor said so, $r$ exists, and thus $f$ does not have 0 roots. Suppose $f$ has 1 root. Then $r=-1/r$, and thus $r^4=1$. Since the only fourth roots of $1$ are $\left\{ \pm 1,\pm i \right\} $, and since none of these are roots of $f$:
\[
	1+1-1-1+1=1,\qquad 1-1-1+1+1=1,
\]
\[
	1-i+1-i+1=3-2i,\qquad 1+i+1+i+1=3+2i
,\]
we get a contradiction. Therefore, $f$ has more than 1 root. This also shows that roots need to come in pairs with \emph{distinct elements}, since $r\neq -1/r$ for $r$ and root, and thus the roots must be an even number. Since $f$ has degree 4, it can have at most 4 roots. Therefore, it has either 2 or 4 roots.
