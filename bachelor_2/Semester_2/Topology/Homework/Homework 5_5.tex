\section*{Problem 1}
Since $f$ is continuous, for all $\varepsilon >0$ and for all $x\in X$, there exists $\delta_x>0$ such that $d_X(x,y)<\delta_x $ implies $d_Y(f(x),f(y))<\varepsilon $. In particular, we can without loss of generality take $\delta_x$ such that $d_Y(f(x),f(y))<\varepsilon /2$. It also follows that
\[
	\bigcup_{x\in X} B_{\delta _x/2} (x)=X
,\]
and thus $\left\{ B_{\delta _x/2}  \right\}_{x\in X} $ is an open cover of $X$. Since $X$ is compact, there exists a finite subcover $\left\{ B_{\delta_i/2}(x_i)  \right\}_{i=1}^n$.

Let $\delta \coloneqq \min \left\{ \frac{\delta _i}{2} \right\}_{i=1}^n$, and note that since the subcover is finite, necessarily $\delta >0$. Let $x,y\in X$ such that $d_X(x,y)<\delta $. Since $\left\{ B_{\delta_i/2} (x_i) \right\}_{i=1}^n$ is a cover of $X$, we have that $x\in B_{\delta _i/2} (x_i)$ for some $i$. That is, $d_X(x,x_i)<\delta_i/2$. It follows that
\begin{align*}
	d_X(y,x_i)&\leq d_X(y,x)+d_X(x,x_i)\\
		  &\leq \delta +\frac{\delta_i}{2} \\
		  &\leq \frac{\delta_i}{2} +\frac{\delta _i}{2} \\
		  &=\delta _i
.\end{align*}
Therefore, $x,y\in B_{\delta _i} (x_i)$, and by our choice of $\delta_i$, we have
\[
	d_Y(f(x),f(x_i))\leq \frac{\varepsilon }{2} ,\qquad d_Y(f(y),f(x_i))\leq \frac{\varepsilon }{2} 
.\]
By the triangle inequality once again, we have
\[
	d_Y(f(x),f(y))\leq d_Y(f(x),f(x_i))+d_Y(f(y),f(x_i))\leq \frac{\varepsilon }{2} =\varepsilon 
.\]
Therefore, for all $\varepsilon $, there exists $\delta $ such that for any $x,y$ satisfying $d_X(x,y)<\delta $, we have $d_Y(f(x),f(y))<\varepsilon $, and $f$ is uniformly continuous.

Let $X$ have the finite compliment topology, and let $\mathcal{U} \coloneqq \left\{ U_\alpha  \right\}_{\alpha \in A} $ be an open cover of $X$. Choose some set, denoted $V_0 \in \mathcal{U} $. Since $\mathcal{U} $ is an open cover, for all $x\in V_0^{c} $, there exists $\alpha $ such that $x\in U_{\alpha } $. For each such $x\in V_0^{c} $, choose precisely one $V_x$ such that $x\in V_x\in \mathcal{U} $. This gives us a new collection $\mathcal{V} \coloneqq \left\{ V_x \right\}_{x\in V_0^{c} }\cup \{V_0\}$. Clearly this collection is finite; we have at most one $V_x$ for each $x\in V_0^{c} $ which is finite, and thus $\left| \mathcal{V}  \right| =\left| V_0^{c}  \right| +1<\infty $.

Note that for any $x\in X$, we either have $x\in V_0\in \mathcal{V} $, or $x\notin V_0$. In the case of the latter, by definition there exists $x\in V_x\in \mathcal{V} $. Therefore, $\mathcal{V} $ is a finite cover of $X$. Since $V_x\in \mathcal{U} $ for all $x$, it is indeed a finite subcover of $X$, and $X$ is compact.

Consider $\mathbb{N} $ with the finite compliment topology, and consider the subset $\mathbb{N} \setminus \left\{ 0 \right\} $. Clearly this space is not closed, and sets are compact if and only if they are compact with respect to the subspace topology. By our previous result, it thus suffices to show $\mathbb{N} \setminus \left\{ 0 \right\} $ has the finite compliment topology. Indeed, let $U\subseteq \mathbb{N}\setminus \left\{ 0 \right\}  $ be open. Then there exists some $U'$ open in $\mathbb{N} $ such that
\[
	U=U' \cap \left( \mathbb{N} \setminus \left\{ 0 \right\}  \right) =U'\setminus \left\{ 0 \right\} 
.\]
If $0\notin U'$, then the compliment of $U$ is simply $U^{\prime c} \setminus \left\{ 0 \right\} $. If $0\in U'$, then $U^{c} =U^{\prime c} $. Since $U^{\prime c} $ is finite, both of these sets are clearly finite, and thus all open sets in $\mathbb{N} \setminus \left\{ 0 \right\} $ have finite compliment.

For the converse, let $U\subseteq \mathbb{N} \setminus \left\{ 0 \right\} $ have finite compliment. Then $U=U \cap \mathbb{N} $, and since $U$ has finite compliment in $\mathbb{N} \setminus \left\{ 0 \right\} $, it's compliment in $\mathbb{N} $ is also clearly finite. Therefore, $U$ is open, and $\mathbb{N} \setminus \left\{ 0 \right\} $ inherits the finite compliment topology.

\begin{lemma}\label{lma:1}
	Let $X$ be compact, $Y$ be Hausdorff, and let $f : X \to Y$ be continuous and bijective. Then $f$ is a homeomorphism.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	First, we show that a bijection $f : X \to Y$ is continuous if and only if the preimage of every closed set is closed. Let $f$ be continuous, and let $C\subseteq Y$ be closed. Then $C^{c} $ is open, and thus $f^{-1} (C^{c} )$ is open. Since $f^{-1} $ is a bijection, $f^{-1} (C)\cup f^{-1} (C^{c} )=X$, and thus $f^{-1} (C^{c} )=f^{-1} (C)^{c} $. Therefore, $f^{-1} (C)^{c} $ is open, and thus $f^{-1} (C)$ is closed.

	For the converse, let $f$ be a bijection such that the preimage of every closed set is closed. Let $U\subseteq Y$ be open, and note that by the same logic as before, $f^{-1} (U^{c} )=f^{-1} (U)^{c} $ is closed. Therefore, $f^{-1} (U)$ is open in $X$, and $f$ is continuous.

	Finally, let $C$ be closed in $X$. Since $X$ is compact and $C$ is closed, we have that $C$ is compact. It's easily shown using previous logic that the restriction $f|_C$ of a continuous function $f$ to a subspace $C$ is still continuous with respect to the subspace topology. Thus, $f|_C : C \to Y$ is continuous, and thus $f|_C(C)=f(C)$ is compact in $Y$. Since $Y$ is Hausdorff, we have that $f(C)$ is closed in $Y$, and thus $f^{-1} $ is continuous by our above argument.
\end{proof}
