\begin{notation}
	If $f : A \to X$ and $g : A \to Y$ are morphisms, then the morphism $f\times g$ will denote the morphism induced by the universal property of products. If $f : A \to X$ and $g : B \to Y$ are morphisms now with \emph{different} domains, then the morphism $f\times g$ is the morphism induced by the product bifunctor; equivalently, it is the induced morphism in the diagram
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
	 & A\times B \ar[" f\circ \pi _A "', dl] \ar[" g\circ \pi _B ", dr]\ar[" f\times g ", dashed, d]  &  \\
	X & X\times Y\ar[" \pi_X ", l] \ar[" \pi _Y "', r]  & Y
	\end{tikzcd}\]
\end{notation}


\section*{Problem 1}
Due to the notation used in the problem statement, I will assume $X$ is path-connected since $\pi _1$ was stated without specifying a base point. This, together with the fact that $D^2$ is path-connected, makes almost all mention of base points immaterial in this problem. At some points when using constant maps it can be implicitly assumed what the base point is.

First, note that loops in $X$ can be equivalently viewed as morphisms $S^1\to X$. To see this explicitly, note that every loop $\varphi : [0,1] \to X$ has $\varphi (0)=\varphi (1)$, and thus it factors uniquely through the quotient
\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
\left[ 0,1 \right] \ar[" \varphi  ", r] \ar[" \pi  "', d]  & X \\
\left[ 0,1 \right] /\tildesim \ar[" \overline{\varphi }  "', dashed, ur]  & 
\end{tikzcd}\]
for $\pi $ the canonical projection, where $\sim $ is the relation $0\sim 1$. It's clear that $\left[ 0,1 \right] /\tildesim \cong S^1$, so every loop $\varphi $ can be equivalently viewed as a continuous map $\overline{\varphi } : S^1 \to X$. To fully formalize this idea up as far as will be needed for this problem, consider the following lemma, which will implicitly be invoked throughout the problem.
\begin{lemma}\label{lma:1}
	With notation as above, $\varphi $ is null-homotopic if and only if $\overline{\varphi } $ is null-homotopic.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	First, let $\varphi $ be null-homotopic to the constant map $x_0$. Since $\varphi $ is a loop, $\varphi (0)=\varphi (1)$. Let $\Phi : \varphi  \Rightarrow x_0$ be the homotopy in question, and define the map
	\[
		\Psi \coloneqq \Phi (x)
	.\]
	This is well defined; that is, for any $x\in[0,1]$, we can take $\Phi (x,0)=\Phi (x,1)$ without loss of generality. This assumption follows from the fact that for any $x$, we can simply concatenate on the left and right by paths that flow from some equivalent start and end points to the start and end points of $\Phi (x,-)$. With this definition, it is exceedingly clear that $\Phi $ now defines a homotopy $\overline{\varphi } \Rightarrow x_0$.

	For the converse, let $\overline{\varphi } $ be null-homotopic to the constant map $x_0$. Since $\pi $ is homotopic to itself by the identity homotopy $1_\pi $, we have a diagram
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 4em, column sep = 4em]
		\left[ 0,1 \right] \ar[" \pi  "{name=p1}, bend left, r] \ar[" \pi  "'{name=p2}, bend right, r]  & \left[ 0,1 \right] /\tildesim \ar[" \overline{\varphi }  "{name=vp}, bend left, r] \ar[" x_0 "'{name=x0}, bend right, r]  & X.
		\arrow[from=p1, to=p2, Rightarrow, shorten=4px]
		\arrow[from=vp, to=x0, Rightarrow, shorten=4px]
	\end{tikzcd}\]
	By lemma \ref{lma:2} (yes, I presented the results out of order. Cope), there is an induced homotopy
	\[
		\varphi =\overline{\varphi } \circ \pi \Rightarrow x_0 \circ \pi =x_0,
	\]
	and thus $\varphi $ is null-homotopic.
\end{proof}


Suppose that every morphism $f : S^1 \to X$ extends to a morphism $ \overline{f} : D^2 \to X$; in other words, the diagram
\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
D^2\ar[" \overline{f}  ", r]  & X \\
S^1 \ar[" \iota  ", hook, u] \ar[" f "', ur]  & 
\end{tikzcd}\]
commutes, with $\iota $ the inclusion $S^1 \hookrightarrow D^2$. This means that every loop $f$ can be viewed as the composition $\overline{f} \circ \iota $. However, note that $\iota $ is a morphism $S^1\to D^2$, and thus a loop in $D^2$. Using the fact that $\pi _1(D^2)=0$, we have that all loops in $D^2$ are null-homotopic, and thus there is a homotopy $\Phi : \left[ 0,1 \right] \times S^1 \to D^2$ with $\Phi (0,-)=\iota $ and $\Phi (1,-)=x_0$, for some fixed $x_0\in D^2$. Additionally, note that $\overline{f} $ is homotopic to itself by the identity homotopy $1_{\overline{f} } : [0,1]\times D^2 \to X$, defined as $1_{\overline{f} }(x,-)= \overline{f} $ for all $x\in D^2$. We thus have homotopies
\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 5em, column sep = 5em]
	S^1 \ar[bend left, " \iota  "{name=i}, r] \ar[bend right, " x_0 "'{name=x0}, r]  & D^2 \ar[bend left, " \overline{f}  "{name=f1}, r] \ar[bend right, " \overline{f}  "'{name=f2}, r]  & X
	\arrow[Rightarrow, from=i, to=x0, shorten=5px]\arrow[Rightarrow, from=f1, to=f2, shorten=5px]
\end{tikzcd}\]
Consider the following lemma.

\begin{lemma}\label{lma:2}
	Homotopies $\Phi_1 : f_1 \Rightarrow g_1$ and $\Phi _2 : f_2 \Rightarrow g_2$ can be composed horizontally; that is, if $g_1 \sim f_1$ and $g_2\sim f_2$ for $\sim$ the homotopy relation, then $g_2\circ g_1\sim f_2\circ f_1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	Let
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 0em, column sep = 2.5em]
	X\ar[" f_1 ", r]  & Y\ar[" g_1 ", r]  & Z, \\
	X\ar[" f_2 "', r]  & Y\ar[" g_2 "', r]  & Z
	\end{tikzcd}\]
	be morphisms, and let $\Phi _1 : [0,1]\times X \to Y$ and $\Phi _2 : [0,1]\times Y \to Z$ be homotopies $f_1 \Rightarrow g_1$ and $f_2 \Rightarrow g_2$, respectively. Define the horizontal composite $\Phi _2 \cdot \Phi _1 : [0,1]\times X \to Z$ as
	\[
		(\Phi _2 \cdot \Phi _1)(x,-)=\Phi _2(x,\Phi _1(x,-))
	.\]
	To show that this is a homotopy $f_2\circ f_1 \Rightarrow g_2\circ g_1$, first note that
	\[
		\Phi _2(0,\Phi _1(0,-))=\Phi _2(0,f_1(-))=f_2(f_1(-))=f_2\circ f_1,	
	\]
	by definition of $\Phi _1$ and $\Phi _2$. Similarly,
	\[
		\Phi _2(1,\Phi _1(1,-))=\Phi _2(1,g_1(-))=g_2\circ g_1
	.\]
	Finally, to show $\Phi _2\cdot \Phi _1$ is continuous, note that we can view $\Phi _2\cdot \Phi _1$ as the composition $\Phi _2 \circ (\pi \times \Phi _1)$, where $\pi $ is the natural projection $\left[ 0,1 \right] \times X\to [0,1]$, and $\pi \times \Phi _1$ is the map induced by the universal property of products
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
	 & \left[ 0,1 \right] \times X \ar[" \pi  "', dl] \ar[" \Phi _1 ", dr] \ar[dashed, " \pi \times \Phi _1 ", d] &  \\
	\left[ 0,1 \right]  & \left[ 0,1 \right] \times Y\ar[l] \ar[r]  & Y
	\end{tikzcd}\]
	Proof of this is immediate:
	\[
		(\Phi _2\circ (\pi \times \Phi _1))(x,-)=\Phi _2(x,\Phi _1(x,-))=\left( \Phi _2\cdot \Phi _1 \right) (x,-)
	.\]
	Since $\pi $ and $\Phi _1$ are continuous, so is $\pi \times \Phi _1$, and thus so is $\Phi _2\circ (\pi \times \Phi _1)=\Phi _2\cdot \Phi _1$. Therefore, horizontal composition of homotopies holds, and $f_2\circ f_1\sim g_2\circ g_1$.
\end{proof}

By lemma \ref{lma:2}, there is a homotopy $\overline{f} \circ \iota \Rightarrow \overline{f} \circ x_0$. Since the composition $\overline{f} \circ x_0$ is clearly still a constant map $x\mapsto \overline{f} (x_0)$, and since $\overline{f} \circ \iota $ is homotopic to $\overline{f} \circ x_0$, we have that $\overline{f} \circ \iota $ is null-homotopic. Since any loop $f$ is equal to such a composition $\overline{f} \circ \iota $ by assumption, we have that every loop $f$ is null-homotopic, and thus $\pi _1(X)\cong 0$.

Now for the converse, let $\pi _1(X)\cong 0$, and let $f : S^1 \to X$ be continuous. Since $f$ can be viewed as a loop, it is necessarily null-homotopic $\Phi :f\Rightarrow x_0$ for $x_0$ the constant map $x\mapsto x_0$ with $x_0$ fixed.

Let $(x_1,y_1)\sim (x_2,y_2)$ iff $x_1=x_2=1$ be a relation on $[0,1]\times S^1$, and note that since $\Phi (1,-)=x_0$, by the universal property of quotients it factors uniquely through the quotient:
\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
\left[ 0,1 \right] \times S^1\ar[" \Phi  ", r]\ar[d]  & X \\
\left[ 0,1 \right] \times S^1/\tildesim \ar[dashed, ur]  & 
\end{tikzcd}\]
For simplicity, define $A\coloneqq [0,1]\times S^1/\tildesim $. We claim that $A\cong D^2$. Note that every element of $A$ is of the form $(x,\cos \theta ,\sin \theta )$ for some $x\in [0,1]$ and $\theta \in [0,2\pi )$. Let $\psi : [0,1]\times S^1 \to D^2$ be the map
\[
	(x,\cos \theta ,\sin \theta )\mapsto (1-x)(\cos \theta ,\sin \theta )
.\]
Since $\psi (1,x,y)=0$, this map identifies all points with $x=1$, it also factors through $A$ and induces a continuous map $\overline{\psi } : A \to D^2$ by mapping $[a]\mapsto \psi (a)$.

Now we show that $\psi $ (not $\overline{\psi } $) is surjective. Given $r(\cos \theta ,\sin \theta )\in D^2$, we have that since $r\in [0,1]$, it can be viewed as $(1-x)$ for some $x\in [0,1]$. It is then manifestly surjective, as for every such $x$, there exists $(x,\cos \theta ,\sin \theta )\in [0,1]\times S^1$. Also note that the map is not injective. However, for any $(x,\cos \theta ,\sin \theta )\neq (y,\cos \phi ,\sin \phi )$, it can easily be shown that
\[
	(1-x)(\cos \theta ,\sin \theta )=(1-y)(\cos \phi ,\sin \phi )\implies x=y=1
\]
simply by our knowledge of $S^1$ and $D^2$. Therefore, $\psi (x,\cos \theta ,\sin \theta )=\psi (y,\cos \phi ,\sin \phi )$ if and only if $x=y=1$. The quotienting relation $\sim $ defining $A$ is thus equivalent to the relation $x\sim y$ iff $\psi (x)=\psi (y)$. The canonical factorization of $\psi $ is precisely it's factorization through $A$:
\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
	\left[ 0,1 \right] \times S^1\ar[" \psi  ", bend left, rrr] \ar[" \pi  "', two heads, r]  & A\ar[" \overline{\psi }  "', dashed, r]  & \im \psi \ar[equals, r] &D^2,
\end{tikzcd}\]
where the fact that $\im \psi = D^2$ follows from the surjectivity of $\psi $, and thus $\overline{\psi } $ is a bijection $A\to D^2$. Consider the following lemma.

\begin{lemma}\label{lma:3}
	Let $X$ be compact. Then the quotient $X/\sim $ is compact for any relation $\sim $.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	Let $\left\{ U_{\alpha }  \right\}_{\alpha \in A} $ be an open cover of $X/\sim $. Since the quotient topology is defined such that the canonical projection $\pi : X \to X/\sim $ is continuous, we have that $\pi ^{-1} (U_\alpha )$ is open in $X$ for all $\alpha $, and thus
	\[
		\bigcup_{\alpha \in A} \pi ^{-1} (U)=X
	\]
	is an open cover. Since $X$ is compact, this admits a finite subcover
	\[
		\bigcup_{i=1} ^n \pi ^{-1} (U_i)=X,
	\]
	and since $\pi $ is manifestly surjective, we have
	\[
		X/\sim =\pi (X)=\pi \left( \bigcup_{i=1}^n \pi ^{-1} (U_i) \right) =\bigcup_{i=1}^n \pi (\pi ^{-1} (U_i))=\bigcup_{i=1}^n U_i
	.\]
	Therefore, $X/\sim $ is compact.
\end{proof}

By lemma \ref{lma:3}, $A$ is compact. Since $D^2$ is Hausdorff, we have that $\overline{\psi }$ is a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space. By a result I proved in the last homework, $\overline{\psi } $ is thus a homeomorphism, and $A\cong D^2$.

Returning to our discussion of the homotopy $\Phi$, we thus have that the diagram
\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
\left[ 0,1 \right] \times S^1\ar[" \Phi  ", r] \ar[d]  & X \\
D^2\ar[dashed," \varphi "',  ur]  & 
\end{tikzcd}\]
commutes. We claim that $\varphi $ is an extension of $f$ to $D^2$. For this to be true, we would need the diagram
\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
D^2\ar[" \varphi  ", dr]  &  \\
S^1\ar[" \iota  ", u] \ar[" f "', r]  & X
\end{tikzcd}\]
to commute. Note that $\Phi (0,-)=f$ since $\Phi $ is a homotopy $f\Rightarrow x_0$. Also note that due to the definition of the homeomorphism $\overline{\psi } $, points $(0,x)\in [0,1]\times S^1$ are identified with the boundary of $D^2$. In other words, the equivalence class of $(0,\cos \theta ,\sin \theta )$ corresponds to $(\cos \theta ,\sin \theta )$. We thus have that any point $x\in D^2$ with radius $1$ must have
\[
	\varphi (x)=\Phi (0,[x])=\Phi (0,x)=f(x)
.\]
Since $\iota (x)$ has radius 1 for any $x\in S^1$, we have
\[
	(\varphi \circ \iota )(x)=f(x),
\]
and thus $\varphi \circ \iota =f$. Therefore, the diagram commutes, and the statement is proven.\hfill$\blacksquare$
\section*{Problem 2}
\begin{notation}
In this problem, $\bullet$ will denote loop concatenation, $\circ $ will denote function composition, and juxtaposition of functions will denote pointwise group multiplication. In general, $1_X$ denotes an identity morphism on $X$, and $e_G$ denotes the identity of a group $G$.
\end{notation}


Let $G$ be a path connected topological group, and since $\pi _1$ is invariant under choice of base point for path connected spaces, we can choose $\pi _1(G)$ to denote $\pi _1(G,e_G)$ without loss of generality.

Let $\alpha ,\beta $ be loops based at $e_G$. We will first show $\alpha \beta $ is a loop. Let $\Delta : [0,1] \to [0,1]\times [0,1]$ be the map $x\mapsto (x,x)$, and let $\mu : G\times G \to G$ be group multiplication. We are given that $\mu $ is continuous, and obviously $\Delta =1_{\left[ 0,1 \right] } \times 1_{\left[ 0,1 \right] } $, so $\Delta $ is also continuous. The identity function $1_G$ is clearly continuous, as it is the identity morphism in both $\mathbf{Top} $ and $\mathbf{Grp} $, and thus we have that $\alpha \times 1_{\left[ 0,1 \right] } $ and $1_{G} \times \beta $ are continuous. Therefore, we can represent the function $\alpha \beta $ as the composition of continuous functions (where $1$ is a shorthand for the relevant identity)
\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
\left[ 0,1 \right] \ar[" \Delta  ", r]\ar[bend left, " \alpha \beta  ", rrrr]  & \left[ 0,1 \right] \times \left[ 0,1 \right] \ar[" \alpha \times 1 ", r]  & G \times \left[ 0,1 \right] \ar[" 1\times \beta  ", r]  & G\times G\ar[" \mu  ", r]  & G,
\end{tikzcd}\]
and thus $\alpha \beta : [0,1] \to G$ is continuous.

Next, note that since $\alpha ,\beta $ are both loops based at $e_G$, we have that
\[
	\alpha(0)\beta (0)=e_G^2=e_G,\qquad \alpha (1)\beta (1)=e_G^2=e_G
\]
Therefore, $\alpha \beta $ is also a loop based at $e_G$.

Let $\alpha _1,\alpha _2,\beta _1,\beta _2$ be loops based at $e_G$. Note that for all $0\leq x\leq 1/2$, we have
\[
	(\alpha _1\bullet \beta _1)(x)\cdot (\alpha _2\bullet \beta _2)(x)=\alpha _1(2x)\cdot \beta _1(2x)
.\]
Similarly, for $1/2\leq x\leq 1$, we have
\[
	(\alpha _1\bullet \beta _1)(x)\cdot (\alpha _2\bullet \beta _2)(x)=\alpha _2(2x-1)\cdot \beta _2(2x-1)
.\]
Note also that for $0\leq x\leq 1/2$, we have
\[
	(\alpha _1\beta _1)\bullet (\alpha _2\beta _2)(2x)=(\alpha _1\beta _1)(x)=\alpha _1(2x)\beta _1(2x),
\]
and for $1/2\leq x\leq 1$, we have
\[
	(\alpha _1\beta _1)\bullet (\alpha _2\beta _2)(2x)=(\alpha _2\beta _2)(x)=\alpha _2(2x-1)\beta _2(2x-1),
.\]
Since $\alpha _i\beta _i$ is a loop for all $i$, we thus have
\[
	(\alpha _1\bullet\beta _1)(\alpha _2\bullet \beta _2)=(\alpha _1\beta _1)\bullet(\alpha _2\beta _2).
\]
In particular,
\[
	(\alpha \bullet e )(e \bullet \beta )=(\alpha e )\bullet (e \beta ),
\]
where $e$ in this case denotes the constant loop at $e_G$. We trivially have
\[
	(\alpha e)\bullet (e\beta )=\alpha \bullet \beta ,
\]
and we also trivially have that
\[
	\alpha \bullet e\sim \alpha ,\qquad e\bullet \beta \sim \beta 
\]
for $\sim $ homotopy, since we already have $e$ is an identity up to homotopy with respect to loop concatenation, simply by our investigtation of $\pi _1$.

Next, note that since we have homotopies
\[
	\alpha \bullet e\Rightarrow \alpha ,\qquad e\bullet \beta \Rightarrow \beta ,
\]
and we have a similar decomposition (where once again $1$ is a shorthand for the relevant identity)
\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
	\left[ 0,1 \right] \ar[" \Delta  ", r] \ar[bend left, " \alpha\bullet \beta  ", rrrr]\ar[bend right, " (\alpha \bullet e)(e\bullet \beta ) "', rrrr]  & \left[ 0,1 \right] \times \left[ 0,1 \right] \ar[" (\alpha \bullet e)\times 1 ", r]  & G\times \left[ 0,1 \right] \ar[" 1\times (\beta \bullet e) ", r]  & G\times G\ar[" \mu  ", r]  & G.
\end{tikzcd}\]
\begin{lemma}\label{lma:4}
	Let
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
	 & X\ar[" f_1 "', shift right, dl] \ar[" g_1 ", shift left, dl] \ar[" f_2 ", shift left, dr] \ar[" g_2 "', shift right, dr]  &  \\
	Y &  & Z
	\end{tikzcd}\]
	be continuous maps, and let
	\[
		\Phi _1 : f_1 \Rightarrow g_1, \qquad \Phi _2 : f_2 \Rightarrow g_2
	\]
	be homotopies. Then there is an induced homotopy
	\[
		\Phi : f_1\times f_2 \Rightarrow g_1\times g_2
	.\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	This is equivalent to showing there exists a homotopy $\Phi $ making the diagram
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
	 &  & X\ar[" f_2 "'{name=f2}, bend left, drr] \ar[" f_1 "'{name=f1}, bend right, dddddll] \ar[" f_1\times f_2 "{name=ff}, dashed, ddd] &  &  &  &  &  & X \ar[" g_2 "'{name=g2}, bend left, drr]\\
	 &  &  &  & Z\ar[equals, rrrrrr]  &  &  &  &  &  & Z \\
	 &  &  &  &  &  &  &  &  \\
	 &  & Y\times Z\ar[equals, rrrrrr]\ar[" \pi _Y ", two heads, ddll]\ar[" \pi _Z "', two heads, uurr] &  &  &  &  &  & Y\times Z\ar[" \pi _Y ", two heads, ddll] \ar[" \pi _Z "', two heads, uurr] \\
	 &  &  &  &  &  &  &  &  \\
	Y\ar[equals, rrrrrr]  &  &  &  &  &  & Y &  & 
	\arrow[from=f2, to=g2, Rightarrow, color=red, shorten=20pt, " \Phi_2 "']
	\arrow[from=1-3, to=1-9, crossing over, equals]
	\arrow[from=1-9, to=4-9, dashed, " g_1\times g_2 "{name=gg}, crossing over]
	\arrow[from=ff, to=gg, " \Phi  ", Rightarrow, shorten=20pt, color=red, crossing over]
	\arrow[from=1-9, to=6-7, " g_1 "'{name=g1}, bend right, crossing over]
	\arrow[from=f1, to=g1, Rightarrow, color=red, crossing over, shorten=20pt, " \Phi_1 "]
	\end{tikzcd}\]
	commute up to the given homotopies. This diagram does not help the proof at all I just though it looked cool. Since the homotopies are morphisms
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 0em]
	 & \left[ 0,1 \right] \times X\ar[" \Phi _2 ", dr] \ar[" \Phi _1 "', dl]  &  \\
	Y &  & Z
	\end{tikzcd},\]
	we can consider the product $\Phi _1\times \Phi _2: [0,1]\times X\times [0,1]\times X \to Y\times Z$, defined as
	\[
		(\Phi _1\times \Phi _2)(t_1,x,t_2,y)=(\Phi _1(t_1,x),\Phi _2(t_2,x))
	.\]
	Define $\Phi : [0,1]\times X\times X \to Y\times Z$ as the composition
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 4em]
		\left[ 0,1 \right] \times X\times X\ar[bend left, " \Phi  ", rrr]\ar[" \Delta\times 1_X\times 1_X ", r]  & \left[ 0,1 \right] \times \left[ 0,1 \right] \times X\times X\ar[" \gamma  ", r]  & \left[ 0,1 \right] \times X\times \left[ 0,1 \right] \times X\ar[" \Phi _1\times \Phi _2 ", r] & Y\times Z,
	\end{tikzcd}\]
	where $\Delta : \left[ 0,1 \right]  \to \left[ 0,1 \right] \times \left[ 0,1 \right] $ is still the map $t\mapsto (t,t)$, and $\gamma$ is the commutator
	\[
		\gamma (t_1,t_2,x,y)=(t_1,x,t_2,y)
	.\]
	More precisely, $\gamma $ is the relevant component of the natural isomorphism $\_\times \_\Rightarrow \_\times \_$ which gives commutivity of products.

	Since $\Phi _1\times \Phi _2$ is the product of continuous functions, it is continuous. Similarly, $\Delta \times 1_X\times 1_X$ is continuous. Finally, $\gamma $ is trivially shown to be continuous, so we have that $\Phi $ is a composition of continuous functions, and thus continuous. It suffices to show $\Phi $ determines a homotopy $f_1\times f_2\Rightarrow g_1\times g_2$. Note that we can explicitly describe $\Phi(t,x,y) $ as
	\begin{align*}
		\left( \left( \Phi _1\times \Phi _2 \right) \circ \gamma \circ \left( \Delta \times 1_X\times 1_X \right)  \right) (t,x,y)&=\left( \left( \Phi _1\times \Phi _2 \right) \circ \gamma  \right) (t,t,x,y)\\
																	  &=\left( \Phi _1\times \Phi _2 \right) (t,x,t,y)\\
																	  &=(\Phi _1(t,x),\Phi _2(t,y))
	.\end{align*}
	We thus have
	\[
		\Phi (0,x,y)=\left( \Phi _1(0,x),\Phi _2(0,y) \right) =(f_1(x),f_2(y))=(f_1\times f_2)(x,y)
	.\]
	Similarly,
	\[
		\Phi (1,x,y)=\left( \Phi _1(1,x),\Phi _2(1,y) \right) =\left( g_1(x),g_2(y) \right) =\left( g_1\times g_2 \right) (x,y)
	.\]
	Therefore, there exists a homotopy $\Phi : f_1\times f_2 \Rightarrow g_1\times g_2$.
\end{proof}

\section*{Problem 3}
Given a morphism $f:(X,x_0)\to (Y,y_0)$, define the induced morphism $f_*\coloneqq \pi _1(f)$. let $\mathrm{id} _X : (X,x_0) \to (X,x_0)$ be the identity morphism, and note that due to our prior knowledge of the category $\mathbf{Grp} $, the identity morphism $\mathrm{id} _G$ on any group $G$ is the map $g\mapsto g$. It thus suffices to show $\mathrm{id}_{X*} $ is the map $\left[ \varphi  \right] \mapsto \left[ \varphi \right] $. By definition,
\[
	\mathrm{id}_{X*} [\varphi ]=\left[ \mathrm{id}_X \circ \varphi  \right] =\left[ \varphi  \right] ,
\]
since $\mathrm{id}_X$ is an identity morphism in $\mathbf{Top} _*$, and the homotopy class of $\varphi $ consists entirely of morphisms in $\mathbf{Top} _*$. Therefore, $\mathrm{id}_{X*} $ is the identity morphism $\mathrm{id}_{\pi _1(X,x_0)} $.

Let
\[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = 2.5em, column sep = 2.5em]
X\ar[" f ", r]  & Y\ar[" g ", r]  & Z
\end{tikzcd}\]
be morphisms in $\mathbf{Top}$, for $X,Y,Z$ path-connected spaces (such that $\pi _1$ is well-defined up to isomorphism for any choice of base point). We have
\[
	(g_*\circ f_*)[\varphi ]=g_*[f\circ \varphi ]=[g\circ (f\circ \phi) ]=[(g\circ f)\circ \varphi ]=(g\circ f)_*[\varphi ]
.\]
Therefore, $\pi _1$ preserves morphism composition, and thus $\pi _1$ is a functor (if we restrict $\mathbf{Top} $ to path connected spaces; I'm pretty sure the proof for functorality $\mathbf{Top} _*\to \mathbf{Grp} $ is the same but that wasn't the problem sooooooo).

Since $\pi _1$ is a functor, if $f : X \to Y$ is a homeomorphism, then it has a two-sided continuous inverse $f^{-1} : Y \to X$. We thus have
\[
	\pi_1(f)\circ \pi _1(f^{-1} )=\pi _1(f\circ f^{-1} )=\pi _1(\mathrm{id}_Y)=\mathrm{id}_{\pi _1(Y)} 
,\]
and similarly $\pi _1(f^{-1} )\circ \pi _1(f)=\mathrm{id}_{\pi _1(X)} $. Therefore, $\pi _1(X)\cong \pi _1(Y)$.\hfill$\blacksquare$
